# Questions the course has already graded

Twelve times across the eight lecture weeks, the course stopped and put a question on the
screen **with a mark value attached** — quiz items answered live in the hall, and written
reports handed in for marks. Those twelve are the clearest signal anyone has about what the
examiner considers worth marking, and they are listed here in course order.

Everything below is **our own wording and our own answer**, with the section of the notes that
teaches it. Nothing from the lecture slides is reproduced. Deadlines, submission formats and
mark weights for the reports come from the course itself and change from year to year — check
those against your own course page, not against this list.

> **How to use this page.** These are not extra questions to grind. Read the answer, then open
> the section behind it — every one of them is a topic the course has already told you it cares
> about, so the surrounding material is where revision time pays best.

## Module 01 · The Scientific Study of Life

### 1. The two principles of experimental setup — open question, **10 marks**

The single most valuable item on this list: a ten-mark open question asking you to name and
briefly explain the two principles that should guide the setup of any experiment.

**The answer, and it is worth learning as two sentences:**

1. **Test only one variable at a time.** If two things differ between your groups, and the
   groups end up different, nothing tells you which one caused it — the extra variable is a
   **confound**.
2. **Use controls to hold every other variable constant.** A control group is treated
   identically to the experimental group *except* for the one variable being tested. Controls
   are the mechanism that eliminates confounds.

Ten marks will not be given for two bare sentences. Define **variable**, **experimental group**,
**control group** and **confound**; then work one example through — a drug trial with a placebo
group, or the widowbird tail-length experiment — showing where each principle was applied.

**Read:** [Module 01 §1.3](#/ch01/learn) — then write it out under Module 01 → Practice →
short answer **B3**, which asks this question in the same words and marks it against a checklist.

### 2. Report — should stem-cell use or cloning be allowed?

A written report arguing a position on whether stem-cell research or cloning should be
permitted, with a diagram as well as text.

**How the marks work here.** This is an *evaluation* question, not a recall question, and the
trap is answering it as though science settles it. Science can establish what stem cells are,
what cloning can and cannot currently do, and what the risks are; **whether it should be
allowed is a moral question, and moral questions are outside what science can answer** —
saying so explicitly, early, is what separates a strong answer from a confident one.

A structure that earns the marks: define stem cells and differentiation → what the technology
can actually do today → the case for → the case against → your position, and the criterion you
used to reach it. The diagram is not decoration: a differentiation tree, or a comparison of
therapeutic and reproductive cloning, carries part of the answer.

**Read:** [Module 01 §1.1](#/ch01/learn) for what science can and cannot answer,
[§1.6](#/ch01/learn) for evaluating claims, and [Module 08 §8.9](#/ch08/learn) for
differentiation and stem cells.

## Module 02 · The Chemistry of Life

### 3. Discussion — why is liquid water denser than ice, and what does that mean for life?

**Why ice is less dense.** In liquid water, hydrogen bonds constantly break and re-form as
molecules jostle past one another, so on average the molecules sit fairly close together. On
freezing, each molecule locks into **four hydrogen bonds held at fixed angles**, and that
geometry builds an **open, cage-like lattice** holding the molecules *further apart* than the
liquid does. Same molecules, more space: ice is about **9% less dense**, so it floats.

**What it means for life.** Lakes and ponds freeze **from the top down**. The ice stays at the
surface and insulates the water beneath, so liquid water — and everything living in it —
survives the winter. If ice sank, a lake would freeze solid from the bottom up. The same
property keeps a liquid ocean under the polar ice, and means the early oceans could never have
been sterilised by freezing.

**The trap:** answering "hydrogen bonds" and stopping. The mark is for the mechanism — the
bonds in ice are **fixed in number and angle**, and it is that rigid open lattice that spaces
the molecules out. Density is about spacing, not about bond strength.

**Read:** [Module 02 §2.10](#/ch02/learn)

## Module 03 · Biological Molecules

### 4. Fill in the blanks — how many amino acids, and how many can you make? **2 marks**

**There are 20 different amino acids. The human body can synthesise 11 of them from scratch;
the other 9 must come from the diet and are called essential amino acids.**

"Essential" does not mean more important — it means essential *to eat*. Foods carrying all nine
are **complete proteins** (meat, dairy, and among plants soy and quinoa); incomplete sources can
be combined, the classic pairing being beans and rice.

**Read:** [Module 03 §3.7](#/ch03/learn)

### 5. Open question — "insulin reacts with glucose to lower blood sugar." **1 mark**

**The statement is false, and the course targets this misconception deliberately.**

Insulin is a protein **hormone** — a messenger. It binds receptors on the surface of muscle and
fat cells and instructs them to take glucose out of the bloodstream and store it. Blood sugar
falls because glucose has been **moved and stored**, not because insulin chemically neutralised
it. Hormones carry information; they are not reactants. Say that in one sentence, then name the
mechanism: receptor binding → signal inside the cell → glucose transporters to the membrane →
uptake.

**Read:** [Module 03 §3.7](#/ch03/learn), and [Module 11 §11.7](#/ch11/learn) for the insulin
receptor as a receptor tyrosine kinase. Module 03's short answer **B4** is the same idea dressed
as an advertisement claiming a supplement "chemically neutralises sugar in your blood".

### 6. Fill in the blank — how many types of base are there in total? **1 mark**

**Five:** adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil. A, G and C appear in both DNA and RNA;
T is DNA-only, U is RNA-only.

**Read:** [Module 03 §3.9](#/ch03/learn)

### 7. Fill in the blank — how many types of nucleotide are there in total? **1 mark**

**Eight** — and the two answers are asked separately for exactly this reason. A nucleotide is
defined by its **sugar as well as its base**, so DNA contributes four (deoxyribose with A, T, G,
C) and RNA contributes four (ribose with A, U, G, C). A, G and C each count **twice**, once with
each sugar. **Five bases, eight nucleotides** — keep them apart.

**Read:** [Module 03 §3.9](#/ch03/learn)

### 8. Report — peptide bonds, phosphodiester bonds, and why nucleic acids carry the information

Three tasks in one: the biological significance of the **peptide bond**, the biological
significance of the **phosphodiester bond**, and then an argument for why life uses nucleic
acids as its information carrier, based on phosphodiester chemistry.

**The peptide bond** links amino acids by dehydration synthesis into a chain whose *sequence* is
the information; it is strong and stable, which is what lets a folded protein hold a precise
shape long enough to do a job. **The phosphodiester bond** links nucleotides through the sugar
and phosphate, building a backbone that is chemically **uniform and directional** (5′ → 3′).

The third part is where the marks concentrate, and the answer is the contrast: **the backbone
is identical all the way along, so it constrains nothing** — any base can sit at any position,
which is what makes an arbitrary message possible. It is strong enough to keep that message
intact, yet the bases themselves are held to their partners only by **hydrogen bonds**, weak
enough to unzip for copying and reading. Information storage needs exactly that combination:
a rigid, meaning-free scaffold carrying a variable, readable message.

**Read:** [Module 03 §3.7](#/ch03/learn) and [§3.9](#/ch03/learn)

## Module 05 · Cell Membranes

### 9. Multiple choice — which statement about phospholipids is true? **1 mark**

**The answer is: phospholipids form a bilayer that encloses the cell.** The examiner left the
key visible on the slide, so this one is settled.

The three wrong options are all the same mistake in different clothes — treating the membrane as
a **solid**. Phospholipids are *not* tightly bound to each other (they drift laterally and swap
places constantly), the structure is *not* rigid (that is why the model is called **fluid**
mosaic), and phospholipids most certainly *do* affect fluidity: the length and saturation of
their tails is one of the two dials a cell uses to set it.

**Read:** [Module 05 §5.3](#/ch05/learn) and [§5.4](#/ch05/learn)

### 10. Report — design an artificial cell that delivers an anti-tumour drug to lung cancer cells

Conceptualise an artificial cell carrying a **water-soluble** anti-tumour compound that finds
and kills lung cancer cells specifically, and describe its structure, design principles and
mechanism.

**The design the course's own material points to,** built from Module 05 in order:

- **Structure:** a **phospholipid bilayer vesicle** — a liposome. It self-assembles for the same
  reason a membrane does: tails away from water, heads facing it.
- **Where the drug goes:** the compound is **water-soluble**, so it travels in the
  **aqueous interior**, not in the hydrophobic core of the bilayer. Say this explicitly — it is the detail
  that shows you understand why the bilayer has the structure it has. A fat-soluble drug would
  ride in the tail region instead.
- **Targeting:** surface **glycoproteins or antibodies** that recognise a marker over-expressed
  on lung tumour cells — the same ID-badge logic as the cell's own carbohydrate coat. Specificity
  is a recognition problem, not a chemistry problem.
- **Entry:** binding triggers **receptor-mediated endocytosis**; the vesicle is taken in, and the
  drug is released inside the target cell rather than into the bloodstream.
- **Why this beats free drug:** healthy cells lack the marker, so dose reaches the tumour and
  systemic toxicity falls.

Mention what could go wrong — immune clearance of the vesicle, markers that healthy tissue also
carries — and the report reads as engineering rather than a wish.

> **This one is already a practice question.** Module 05's extended question **C3** is the same
> brief in exam form — spontaneous assembly, why the water-soluble drug stays inside, what to
> change when the vesicle leaks at body temperature, and how to make it enter tumour cells only.
> Work through C3 and its model answer, and the report is most of the way written.

**Read:** [Module 05 §5.3](#/ch05/learn), [§5.6](#/ch05/learn), [§5.13](#/ch05/learn), and
Module 05 → Practice → Part C, question C3.

## Module 06 · The Energy of Life

### 11. Report — the maximum and minimum NET ATP yield from one glucose molecule

Note the word the course highlighted: **NET**, meaning total ATP produced **minus** the ATP
spent to produce it.

**Maximum net yield: 32 ATP. Minimum net yield: 30 ATP.**

The whole report is the ledger, so show it rather than asserting the number: glycolysis produces
4 and consumes 2; pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle deliver **10 NADH and 2 FADH₂**
in total; the electron transport chain values them at **2.5 and 1.5 ATP** respectively. The
difference between 32 and 30 is the **shuttle cost of getting glycolytic NADH into the
mitochondrion** — a cost some tissues pay and some do not.

And note the floor: with **no oxygen at all**, fermentation leaves only the **2 net ATP** from
glycolysis. If the question is read as "minimum across all conditions" rather than "minimum
aerobic", say both numbers and name the condition each belongs to — an answer that names its
assumption cannot be marked wrong for choosing the other reading.

**Read:** [Module 06 §6.10](#/ch06/learn), with [§6.11](#/ch06/learn) for the anaerobic floor.
Module 06's extended question **C1** ("The energy audit") is four students arguing over 34, 32,
30 and 2 — working it settles every version of this question.

## Module 12 · Health & Diseases

### 12. Multiple choice, select all — what makes *M. tuberculosis* resistant to drying and phagocytosis? **1 mark**

**The two keyed answers are the lipid-rich cell wall and the branching rod structure** — the
examiner left both marked on the slide, so give both.

**The mechanism, so you can defend the answer:** the cell wall of *M. tuberculosis* is unusually
**rich in lipids** (waxy mycolic acids). That one structural feature does the work in both
directions — it makes the bacillus **resistant to drying**, so it survives in dust and dried
sputum and transmits through time as well as distance, and it makes it **resistant to
phagocytosis**, so a macrophage that engulfs it may end up carrying it rather than killing it.
It is also why TB treatment runs to six months of multiple drugs.

**Worth knowing where the biology and the mark scheme part company:** the lipid wall is the
cause. The **branching rod structure** is a description of the organism's shape rather than a
mechanism of resistance — the course groups the two together, so answer with both, but do not
carry away the idea that shape is what defeats a macrophage.

**Why the other two options are wrong:** *M. tuberculosis* is
**non-motile — it has no flagella**, and it does **not form spores**. Both distractors are
properties of other bacteria grafted onto this one.

**Read:** [Module 12 §12.21](#/ch12/learn), with [§12.4](#/ch12/learn) for cell wall structure.
Module 12's extended question **C4** puts the same organism into a dormitory outbreak, which is
the form an exam is more likely to use than a bare list of properties.
