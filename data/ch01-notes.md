## 1.1 What science is — and what it is not

**Science is an intellectual activity involving observation, description, experimentation and explanation of the natural world.** The natural world means the objective, measurable world around us — things that can be observed and quantified by anyone, not just believed by someone.

Two clarifications matter more than the definition itself:

> **Science is not a collection of facts — it is something you *do*.** The facts in a textbook are the *products* of science. Science itself is the process that produced them: observing, questioning, testing, revising. That is why this chapter is about method before it is about biology.

> **Science cannot answer questions about immeasurable things.** Religion, morals, ethics, values, art — whether an action is *right*, whether a painting is *beautiful* — are outside science's reach, because there is nothing objective to measure. Science can tell you *what happens* if a policy is adopted; it cannot tell you whether adopting it is *good*. Exam questions like to test this boundary.

A useful mental model: **being a scientist is like being a detective.** A detective answers questions about crimes; a scientist answers questions about the natural world. Does cell-phone radiation cause brain tumours? Does vitamin C prevent colds? Which parent determines a baby's sex? A good detective gathers evidence step by step, looks for patterns, and relies on logic, evidence and objectivity — never a hunch. Bad detectives who rely on hunches imprison innocent people; bad scientists who rely on hunches publish false conclusions. The cure for both is the same: a disciplined method.

**Why this matters to you even if you never work in a lab:** biology-related decisions fill everyday life — should we produce and eat genetically modified organisms? Should we allow stem-cell research or cloning? Is this supplement worth buying? You will vote on these issues, spend money on them, and make health decisions based on them. The point of this chapter is to give you the tools to evaluate the evidence yourself.

## 1.2 The scientific method, step by step

The scientific method is a loop of six steps. Learn the order — questions test it directly.

```
observation → question → hypothesis → prediction → experiment → conclusion
                              ↑ __________________________________ |
                                 (rejected? form a new hypothesis)
```

| Step | What it is | Car example |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | Something you notice | My car won't start |
| **Question** | What you want explained | Why won't my car start? |
| **Hypothesis** | A tentative, **testable** explanation | The battery is dead |
| **Prediction** | An **if/then** statement derived from the hypothesis | *If* the battery is dead, *then* the car will start with a new battery |
| **Experiment** | A test that collects data | Install a new battery and turn the key |
| **Conclusion** | Hypothesis supported or rejected | Starts → supported. Doesn't → rejected; new hypothesis (out of fuel?) |

### Hypothesis vs. prediction — the distinction students lose marks on

- A **hypothesis** is a tentative **explanation** for an observation: "My car won't start *because the battery is dead*." It proposes a cause.
- A **prediction** is a consequence you derive from it: "**If** my hypothesis is true, **then** ___ will happen." It proposes an observable outcome.

One hypothesis can generate several predictions. From the dead-battery hypothesis: (1) my car will start if I install a new battery; (2) a *different* car won't start if I put *my* battery into it. Both are legitimate tests of the same explanation.

**The one non-negotiable property of a hypothesis is that it must be testable.** "My car won't start because it is cursed" is not a scientific hypothesis — no observation could ever disprove it.

### Worked example — the toaster

Observation: my toaster doesn't toast my bread. Question: why doesn't my toaster work? Hypothesis: something is wrong with the electrical outlet. Prediction: *if* something is wrong with the outlet, *then* my coffeemaker also won't work when plugged into it. Experiment: plug the coffeemaker into the outlet. Result: **the coffeemaker works.**

Is the hypothesis supported? **No — it is rejected.** The prediction said the coffeemaker would *fail*; it worked instead, so the outlet is fine and the fault must lie in the toaster itself. Time for a new hypothesis. Notice that a rejected hypothesis is not a failed experiment — the experiment did its job perfectly. It told you where the problem *isn't*.

> **Rejection is progress.** The method is a loop, not a straight line: a rejected hypothesis sends you back to form a better one. Most of science is exactly this — proposing explanations and eliminating the wrong ones.

## 1.3 Designing a good experiment

This is the highest-yield section of the chapter. The lecturer flagged it with a 10-point graded question: *"Generally, during setting up an experiment, we should keep in mind 2 principles. Please briefly explain them."*

> ### The two principles of experimental setup — memorise these word for word
> **1. Test only ONE variable at a time.** A variable is any changeable characteristic of an experiment. If two things differ between your groups, and the groups end up different, you cannot tell which one caused it — the extra variable is a **confound**.
> **2. Use controls to keep every other variable the same between groups.** A control group is treated identically to the experimental group *except* for the one variable being tested. Controls are what eliminate confounds.

The two principles are really one idea seen from two sides: principle 1 says what you may change (one thing); principle 2 says how you guarantee it (hold everything else constant).

### The vocabulary of a good experiment

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Variable** | Any changeable characteristic of an experiment |
| **Experimental group** | Receives the treatment — the one variable being tested |
| **Control group** | Identical in every way *except* the tested variable |
| **Confound** | A second, uncontrolled variable that could explain the result |
| **Placebo** | A fake pill or substance with **no active ingredient** |
| **Blind design** | Subjects don't know which group they're in; **double-blind**: the researchers assessing them don't know either |
| **Randomisation** | Subjects assigned to groups by chance, so groups start out equivalent |
| **Sample size** | The number of subjects — larger samples make chance results less likely |

**Why the placebo exists:** people who *believe* they are being treated often improve simply because of that belief — the placebo effect. If the control group got nothing while the experimental group got a pill, the two groups would differ in *two* ways: the active ingredient *and* the belief of being treated. That is a confound. Giving the control group an identical-looking fake pill removes it, isolating the active ingredient as the only difference.

### Worked example — the echinacea trial

Claim to test: the herb echinacea reduces the duration and severity of the common cold.

- **Hypothesis:** echinacea reduces the duration of the common cold.
- **Prediction:** *if* true, *then* individuals taking echinacea will have less frequent and shorter cold symptoms than individuals who don't.
- **Experiment:** researchers **randomly** divided **437 volunteers** into groups. Each group received either **echinacea or an identical-looking placebo** — that is the one variable under test. **All groups were then exposed to the same cold-causing virus** at the same time, and doctors monitored everyone for five days.

Count the design features: one tested variable (echinacea); a placebo so no subject knows their group; the *same* virus for everyone at the *same* time; identical monitoring — every other variable controlled. The randomisation and large sample (437) mean the groups didn't differ systematically at the start.

> **A note on the real trial, for the curious.** The published study was slightly more elaborate than the version above: it also varied *when* the tablets were given (some groups started seven days before virus exposure, some only after), which makes it a **factorial** design testing two things at once rather than one. That is a legitimate and common design — but it is not the "one variable at a time" ideal this section is teaching, so the simplified two-group version above is the one to reproduce in an exam answer.

- **Results:** all four groups were equally likely to catch the cold, and symptoms lasted equally long in every group.
- **Conclusion:** echinacea had **no effect** on the duration or severity of the common cold. Hypothesis rejected — and because the design was clean, the rejection is trustworthy.

### A second worked example — Andersson's widowbirds

Field biology uses the same two principles. Malte Andersson asked whether the extravagantly long tails of male widowbirds attract females. He shortened the tails of one group of males, lengthened another group's tails (by gluing on the cut feathers), and — crucially — kept **control groups**: birds whose tails were cut and re-glued at the original length, and birds left entirely alone. Those controls matter because they separate the tested variable (tail *length*) from confounds (the stress of being caught, the cutting and gluing itself). Result: lengthened-tail males attracted the most mates. Because only tail length varied, the conclusion — females prefer longer tails — stands.

## 1.4 Repeatability and peer review — why one study is never enough

### Repeatability

**Once is not enough.** An experiment must be repeated several times — ideally by different researchers — before its conclusion is considered valid and widely accepted. Small studies can mislead through chance, small samples or hidden flaws, and larger, better-controlled follow-ups often overturn them.

Real cases from the lectures:

- A **small 2005 study** suggested an epilepsy drug might cure AIDS, generating enormous excitement. A **huge 2008 study** showed it did not work at all.
- Two studies on marijuana and brain structure, published only a few months apart, reached **opposite conclusions** — one found a correlation, the other found none. If either had been treated as final, half of readers would have been misled.

The practical rule: **if a few small studies show one thing but the vast majority of studies show another, go with the majority.** A single contradictory study is often just an outlier — a data point thrown off by chance or error, not a revolution.

### Peer review

**Before results are published, other scientists evaluate the validity of the methods, data and conclusions.** If they are not appropriate and well done, the work is not accepted for publication. Peer review is the quality filter of science — it is why a claim in a reviewed journal carries more weight than a claim in an advertisement.

Two caveats worth knowing:

1. **Not all journals require peer review these days**, which makes it hard for non-scientists to know which results to trust. "Published" no longer automatically means "checked".
2. Peer review also fights **bias**: many journals hide author names during review, because studies show female authors are less likely to have papers accepted when reviewers know their gender — even though most reviewers sincerely believe they are not biased. Bias is usually subconscious, which is exactly why procedures, not good intentions, are needed to counter it.

## 1.5 Hypothesis vs. theory, and basic vs. applied science

### "Theory" means the opposite of what everyday English suggests

In casual speech, "just a theory" means a guess. In science it means almost the reverse:

| | Hypothesis | Scientific theory |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | One tentative explanation for **one** observation | A broad explanation for a **wide range** of phenomena |
| Evidence | Not yet tested, or tested a little | Supported by a **large body** of repeated, independent evidence |
| Example | "My car won't start because the battery is dead" | Cell theory; the theory of evolution; germ theory of disease |

> **A theory is the strongest statement science makes** — an explanation tested so many times, in so many ways, that it would take extraordinary new evidence to overturn it. When someone dismisses evolution as "just a theory", they are using the everyday meaning to attack the scientific one. Gravity is "just a theory" in exactly the same sense.

A theory is still not a proven fact — science never proves anything with absolute finality; every conclusion stays open to revision if strong new evidence appears. That openness is a strength, not a weakness: it is what lets science correct its own mistakes.

### Basic vs. applied science

- **Basic science** seeks knowledge for its own sake, with no immediate practical goal — e.g. cataloguing every gene in the human genome, or studying how bacteria defend themselves against viruses.
- **Applied science** uses accumulated knowledge to solve a specific practical problem — e.g. developing a vaccine, a pest-resistant crop, or a diagnostic test.

The two are not rivals; **applied science is built on basic science.** Basic research into bacterial immune systems looked useless for decades — then it became CRISPR gene editing. Nobody can predict which piece of basic knowledge will become tomorrow's technology, which is the standard argument for funding research with no obvious application.

## 1.6 Evaluating claims — pseudoscience, bias and people who want your money

Science literacy is mostly defence. Four things to watch for:

### 1. Pseudoscience

**Pseudoscience = practices that claim to be factual but are incompatible with the scientific method.** Astrology, crystal healing, detox foot pads — they borrow the *look* of science (jargon, diagrams, "studies show…") without the substance (testable hypotheses, controlled experiments, peer review). The give-away is usually that no conceivable result would make the believers give the claim up — the claims are unfalsifiable or the failures are always explained away.

### 2. Cognitive bias

**Cognitive bias = a mental shortcut that influences our thinking, leading us to process information selectively and subjectively, often producing inaccurate or irrational judgments.** Everyone has them — including scientists — and they are usually subconscious. A lecture example: a student who re-measured a result whenever it *didn't* fit his hypothesis, but measured only once when it did. He wasn't lying; he genuinely felt the inconvenient numbers were "errors". That is why good experimental design (blinding, controls, fixed procedures) exists: **it protects the experiment from the experimenter.**

### 3. People who want your money

**Case 1 — Airborne.** For over 15 years the supplement Airborne claimed to "boost your immune system", citing a "rigorous clinical study". A class-action lawsuit revealed **no study had ever been done — it was completely made up.** The company had to remove the claim, yet sales barely dropped, because most consumers never heard about the lawsuit. Truth: Airborne does not reduce how often you get sick or how long you stay sick.

**Case 2 — Wakefield and the MMR vaccine.** In 1998 Andrew Wakefield published data claiming the MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) vaccine caused autism. It was later found that **he had fabricated the results entirely.** His motives were financial: he had **patented a rival vaccine** he wanted people to buy instead, and he was **being paid by a solicitor** acting for parents of autistic children who wanted grounds to sue vaccine makers. The paper was fully retracted; Wakefield lost his medical licence and his appointments.

**But the damage outlived the fraud.** Some parents still refuse vaccination based on his fake results, and diseases once nearly eradicated — measles, mumps, rubella, whooping cough — have made a comeback as vaccination rates fell. These diseases can be fatal or cause birth defects. One fabricated study, properly debunked, still kills people decades later. This is why the checks in this chapter matter.

### 4. Sensationalism

**Reporters may exaggerate findings to get clicks, or they may have good intentions but too little knowledge to report accurately.** "Scientists cure cancer in mice" becomes "CURE FOR CANCER FOUND". Whenever a headline surprises you, ask: What was the sample size? Was there a control group? Was it peer reviewed? Has it been repeated? — the checklist this chapter has given you.

> **The evaluation checklist:** Is the claim testable? Was there a controlled experiment with a placebo? Was the sample large and randomised? Was it peer reviewed? Has it been independently repeated? Who profits if I believe it?

## 1.7 What makes something alive — the characteristics of life

Here is a surprise: **biology has no simple definition of life.** You cannot define life by its parts — put proteins, fats and DNA together in a test tube and you have all the ingredients, but nothing alive. Instead, scientists agree on a set of **characteristics of life**, and a thing must have **all of them** to count as living.

**LHS = the scientific study of living things for health.** Biology is the scientific study of life; this course focuses it on human health.

| Characteristic | Meaning | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Organised** | Built from atoms → molecules → cells | From bacteria to trees to humans, all life is organised the same way |
| **2. Requires energy & nutrients** | Energy from the **sun** or from **consuming other organisms**; nutrients from the nonliving environment or from other organisms | Nutrients **cycle** within an ecosystem; energy **flows through** — it enters by photosynthesis and leaves mostly as heat |
| **3. Maintains homeostasis** | Regulates its internal state, keeping it constant even as the environment changes | e.g. body temperature, blood pressure, pH. "Life = negentropy" — living things maintain order against decay |
| **4. Grows & develops** | DNA guides development and the expression of traits | Every human grew from a single cell |
| **5. Reproduces** | Transmission of DNA from generation to generation, **using the organism's own machinery** | Required at the **species** level, not the individual level — a childless person is fully alive |
| **6. Evolves** | Change in the **genetic make-up of a population** over multiple generations | Populations evolve; **individuals do not** |

Three details in that table are exam traps, so take them slowly:

- **Reproduction is a species-level requirement — but it must be the organism's own.** A mule (sterile) and a childless human are alive; the criterion is that *some members of the species* reproduce, using their own cellular machinery. Both halves matter: the species-level half is what keeps a sterile individual alive, and the "own machinery" half is what excludes viruses below.
- **Evolution never happens to an individual.** You will not evolve, no matter how long you live. Evolution is a shift in a *population's* genes across *generations*.
- **All characteristics are required.** A crystal grows and is organised, but it has no metabolism, no homeostasis, no DNA-based reproduction — not alive. Fire consumes energy, grows and "reproduces", but has no cells, no homeostasis, no heredity — not alive. Apply the same rule to a **virus** and the verdict is clear: it has organised parts and it evolves, but it has no cells, no metabolism, no homeostasis, and it cannot copy its genes without commandeering a host cell's machinery. It fails several requirements, so **it is not alive** by this definition. (What people mean when they call viruses "debatable" is not that this rule gives an unclear answer — it is that some biologists argue the *rule itself* is drawn in the wrong place, since a virus does carry genes and does evolve. That is an argument about the definition, not an application of it.)

## 1.8 Levels of biological organisation

Characteristic 1 — organisation — deserves its own ladder. Life is structured in nested levels, each built from the one below:

```
atom → molecule → organelle → cell → tissue → organ → organ system
     → organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere
```

| Level | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Atom** | Smallest unit of an element | Carbon atom |
| **Molecule** | Atoms joined by chemical bonds | DNA, water |
| **Organelle** | Structure with a job inside a cell | Nucleus |
| **Cell** | **The smallest unit of life** | A skin cell; a whole bacterium |
| **Tissue** | Group of similar cells with a common function | Muscle tissue |
| **Organ** | Different tissues working together | Heart |
| **Organ system** | Organs cooperating on one task | Circulatory system |
| **Organism** | An individual living thing | You |
| **Population** | All individuals of **one species** in one area | All the pigeons in one city |
| **Community** | **All species'** populations in one area | The pigeons, trees, insects, bacteria… |
| **Ecosystem** | The community **plus the nonliving environment** | …plus the soil, water, air, sunlight |
| **Biosphere** | Every ecosystem on Earth | The living planet |

> **The cell is the smallest unit of life.** Atoms and molecules are not alive — they are chemistry. Life begins at the cell, which is why a bacterium (one cell) is alive but a DNA molecule is not.

Two boundary lines get tested: **population vs. community** (one species vs. all species) and **community vs. ecosystem** (living things only vs. living + nonliving environment). Anchor examples in your memory: add more species → community; add the soil and sunlight → ecosystem.

## 1.9 Naming and sorting life — taxonomy and the three domains

*(The Week 1 lectures barely touch this, but OpenStax Chapter 1 and general exams do — so we teach it.)*

**Taxonomy is the science of classifying and naming organisms.** Organisms are sorted into nested groups, from broadest to most specific:

```
Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species
```

(Mnemonic: **D**ear **K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup.) Each level down contains organisms that are more closely related.

### The three domains

At the very top, all life falls into **three domains**:

| Domain | Cell type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | Prokaryotic (no nucleus) | Single-celled; the familiar microbes |
| **Archaea** | Prokaryotic (no nucleus) | Single-celled; many live in extreme environments (hot springs, brine); chemically distinct from bacteria |
| **Eukarya** | **Eukaryotic** (cells with a nucleus) | Everything else: protists, fungi, plants, animals — including you |

Prokaryotes (Bacteria + Archaea) are simple cells without a nucleus; eukaryotes keep their DNA inside a membrane-bound nucleus. That single cellular difference is the deepest divide in the living world — deeper than the difference between a plant and an animal, which are both Eukarya.

### Binomial nomenclature

Every species gets a two-part Latin name: **Genus + species**, e.g. *Homo sapiens*. Conventions: the genus is capitalised, the species is not, and the whole name is italicised (or underlined when handwritten). The two-part name is unique worldwide, which is the point — "robin" means different birds in Britain and America, but a Latin binomial means exactly one species everywhere.

## Common mistakes in this chapter

1. **Confusing hypothesis with prediction.** The hypothesis is the proposed *explanation*; the prediction is the *if/then consequence* you test.
2. **Calling a rejected hypothesis a failed experiment.** The experiment succeeded — it eliminated a wrong explanation. Reject, revise, retest.
3. **Writing one principle of experimental design instead of two.** (1) Test only one variable at a time; (2) use controls to keep all other variables the same. The 10-point graded question asks for both, briefly explained.
4. **Thinking the placebo is given to the experimental group.** The *control* group gets the placebo, so belief-in-treatment is equal across groups.
5. **Using "just a theory" as if theories were guesses.** In science a theory is a broad, heavily tested explanation — stronger than a hypothesis, not weaker.
6. **Saying individuals evolve.** Evolution is genetic change in a *population* over *generations*.
7. **Counting a thing as alive because it has *some* characteristics of life.** Fire and crystals grow; neither is alive. All characteristics are required.
8. **Mixing up population/community/ecosystem.** One species → population; all species → community; plus the nonliving environment → ecosystem.
9. **Trusting a single dramatic study.** Repeatability first; when studies conflict, go with the weight of the majority.

### Check yourself — answers

1. **Science cannot answer moral questions.** Right and wrong are not measurable properties of the natural world; science addresses only what can be observed and quantified. It can describe stealing's consequences, not its morality.
2. **It is a prediction** — an observable outcome, effectively an if/then. The matching hypothesis is the proposed explanation, e.g. "Red light stimulates stem elongation more than white light." The prediction follows: *if* that is true, *then* plants under red light will grow taller.
3. **The hypothesis ("the outlet is faulty") is rejected** — the outlet demonstrably works. Next step: form a new hypothesis (e.g. "the toaster's heating element is broken") and derive a new testable prediction. The loop continues.
4. To remove the **placebo effect** as a confound. People who believe they are treated often improve from belief alone. With a fake tablet, both groups believe equally, so the only difference between groups is echinacea itself — the one tested variable.
5. **(1) Test only one variable at a time** — changing more than one creates confounds, making it impossible to attribute the result. **(2) Use controls to keep every other variable identical between groups** — the control group differs from the experimental group only in the tested variable, eliminating confounds.
6. That control separates **tail length** (the tested variable) from the **handling itself** (catching, cutting, gluing) — if handling alone affected mating success, this group would reveal it. It keeps the experiment to one variable.
7. **Theory > hypothesis** in breadth and weight of evidence. A hypothesis explains one observation tentatively; a theory explains a wide range of phenomena and is backed by a large body of repeated evidence. Germ theory is a **scientific theory**.
8. **Yes, alive.** Reproduction seems to fail — but that characteristic applies at the **species level**, not the individual level. Horses and donkeys (and mules' parent species) reproduce; an individual's sterility does not disqualify it.
9. **Population** (one species) → **community** (all species) → **ecosystem** (community + nonliving environment).
10. **Bacteria and Archaea** are the prokaryotic domains. Humans: ***Homo sapiens*** — genus capitalised, species lowercase, whole name italicised.
